Case 02 — Consulting & Diagnosis

Where did 420 basis points go?

A last-mile carrier grew revenue 18% over six quarters and lost 420 basis points of gross margin doing it. The CEO blamed fuel. The board blamed pricing. Both were partly right, and neither was the answer.

DisciplineProfitability diagnosis
MethodsMECE bridge · Cost to serve
StructureHypothesis-led
Read8 minutes
01

Situation, complication, question

NorthRoute Logistics runs first- and last-mile delivery across a regional network: roughly 5.3 million drops a month, $105M of quarterly revenue. For two years the story was growth. Then the gross margin started sliding, and it did not stop.

  • Situation. Revenue up 18% across six quarters. Every commercial metric the board watches is green.
  • Complication. Gross margin fell from 24.1% to 19.9%. Gross profit is flat in absolute dollars — the company has added a fifth of its revenue and none of its profit.
  • Question. Is this a cost problem the operations team can fix, or a revenue problem only the commercial team can?

The instinct in the room was to go hunting for cost savings, because that is the lever an operator controls. But a margin decline during a growth phase has a specific smell to it, and it is usually not diesel.

Method note

NorthRoute is an illustrative composite. The structure is what matters: a decomposition that sums exactly to the observed change, and a segment build that reconciles independently to the same margin. Both run live below — the bridge and the cost-to-serve model are not two pictures of one story, they are two calculations that have to agree.

02

Decompose before you diagnose

The first job is arithmetic, not insight. Break the 420bps into pieces that are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, and make them sum to exactly 420 — not "roughly", not "the rest is other". If the bridge does not close, the story built on it is fiction.

Six drivers, isolated by holding everything else at prior-period levels:

◆ Gross margin bridge — Q1 to Q6 Hover any bar for the exact contribution.

Route density is the only thing working in the company's favour — and it is being outrun four to one.

Fuel and linehaul cost 95bps. Real, worth fixing, and nowhere near the whole story. Labour productivity another 70. The single largest line is customer mix at −240bps, and mix is not an operating failure. Mix is the arithmetic consequence of which customers the sales team signed.

This is not a cost problem wearing a revenue costume. It is a revenue problem the cost line is reporting.

Which gives a testable hypothesis: growth has been concentrated in a segment that does not cover its own cost to serve. That is falsifiable — build the segment P&L and either a below-breakeven segment exists or it does not.

03

The segment that ate the margin

Company-level margin is an average, and averages hide exactly the thing you are looking for. Rebuilt bottom-up — revenue per drop against fully-loaded cost per drop, by segment — the picture stops being ambiguous.

◆ Cost to serve by segment Bubble size is quarterly revenue. Dashed outline is the segment in question.

Five segments sit above breakeven. One sits below it — and it is the second-largest by volume in the network.

E-comm returns is 14% of revenue, 1.18 million drops a month, and loses 45 cents on every one of them. It is the segment that grew fastest. Strip it out entirely and group margin would be 24.9% — +509 bps, more than the entire decline the board is asking about.

So why is it so expensive? Not linehaul, and not labour rates. The cost stack shows it:

◆ Cost stack per drop Same four cost pools across every segment.

Failed-delivery rework runs about 35¢ a drop across the network. On E-comm returns it is $1.05 — three times everyone else, on the thinnest revenue per drop in the book.

That is the mechanism. Returns pickups need someone home with a package ready. The first-attempt success rate is far below a normal delivery, so a meaningful share of drops get made twice and paid for once. Every incremental returns contract the sales team signed added revenue, volume, and negative contribution simultaneously.

The root cause is not in operations

Sales commission at NorthRoute is paid on revenue. E-comm returns is the easiest contract in the portfolio to win, because nobody else wants it. The compensation plan was quietly paying people to destroy margin, and every individual involved was behaving rationally. The incentive built the problem; operations just reported it.

04

Re-price, don't exit

The tempting move is to kill the segment. The model says that is worth +509bps, which is more than the problem — and that is precisely why it should be treated with suspicion. Those 1.18 million monthly drops pay for route density that every other segment rides on. Remove them and the +125bps density benefit in the bridge starts unwinding.

Re-pricing is the sharper instrument, and the question becomes how much volume you are willing to lose. Try it:

At a −1.2 elasticity, a 15–18% increase turns the segment contribution positive and buys back roughly 220–260bps of group margin while shedding about a fifth of the volume. That churn is not a side effect to be minimised. It is the mechanism — the contracts that walk are the ones that were never worth serving.

05

What I would put in the readout

  1. Re-price E-comm returns by 15%, on 90 days' notice, in one move. Expect to lose around 18% of that volume and gain roughly 220bps of group margin. Phasing it invites renegotiation on every contract; doing it once makes it a policy rather than an opening offer.
  2. Attack the 70-cent rework gap in parallel. Mandatory delivery windows and a pickup authorisation code on returns contracts. If first-attempt success moves to network average, the segment clears breakeven on its own and the price rise becomes upside instead of survival.
  3. Change the commission basis from revenue to contribution. Everything else here is a one-time correction. This is the only recommendation that stops the problem recurring, and it will be the most unpopular thing in the room.
  4. Model the density unwind before touching volume. The +125bps scale benefit is real and it is load-bearing. Route-level simulation first, then decide how much churn the network can absorb without the cost base stepping back up.

How I would know if I am wrong

  • If first-attempt success on returns is already at network average, the rework story collapses and the problem is simply underpricing — a cleaner fix, and a smaller one.
  • If churn lands materially above 25%, density unwinds faster than the price gain accrues and the net effect could be negative for two to three quarters.
  • If the returns contracts carry volume commitments on other segments, the segment P&L is understating their value and the whole recommendation needs rebuilding at customer level rather than segment level.

Reconciliation

The bridge lands at 19.90%. The independent segment build lands at 19.82% — an 8bp gap. In a live engagement I would chase that down rather than round it away; the most likely culprit is period timing on the rework accrual. Leaving a visible gap is better than forcing a tie.
