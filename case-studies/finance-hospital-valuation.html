Case 01 — Finance & Valuation

What is a hospital system actually worth?

A regional operator is on the block at $1.45B. I built the DCF, ran it 10,000 times, then checked what a private-equity buyer could pay. The three answers disagree — and the disagreement is the entire deal.

01

The question on the table

Meridian Health Partners is a four-hospital regional system: roughly 1,100 licensed beds, $1.18B of revenue, and a 13.5% EBITDA margin. The owners want $1.45B for it — about 9.1× trailing EBITDA. Two very different buyers are circling: a larger health system that already operates in the next county, and a private-equity sponsor running a healthcare services platform.

The naive version of this question is "is $1.45B too much?" The useful version is "too much for whom, and how confident can we be?" Those turn out to have different answers, and the space between them is where the negotiation actually lives.

Read those four numbers together and the case makes itself. The point estimate says yes by three percent. Everything else says that three percent is noise.

Method note — where the numbers come from

Meridian is an illustrative composite, not a real company. Its operating profile is built to sit inside the ranges published for US community hospital systems: mid-teens EBITDA margins, 5–6% of revenue reinvested as capex, and precedent transactions clearing in the 7.5–10× EBITDA band. I have used a composite deliberately — inventing financials for a named real hospital and presenting them as fact would be worse than useless.

Everything downstream of those inputs is real work: the discounting, the terminal-value treatment, the correlation structure in the simulation and the debt schedule all run live in your browser. Move a slider and every number, chart and verdict on this page recomputes.

02

How the model is put together

Free cash flow

Revenue compounds at 4.2%. The EBITDA margin walks linearly from today's 13.5% to a terminal 15.0% by year five — the throughput and labour-cost story a buyer would underwrite. From there, unlevered free cash flow is the usual subtraction:

UFCF = EBITDA − cash taxes − capex − Δ working capital, with cash taxes levied on EBIT so depreciation still earns its shield. Hospitals are capital-hungry, so capex at 5.5% of revenue eats most of the D&A add-back — which is exactly why hospital EBITDA multiples look cheap next to the cash they actually throw off.

Discount rate

An 8.5% WACC, built rather than assumed: a 4.2% risk-free rate, a 5.0% equity risk premium on a 0.95 levered beta, plus 150bps of size premium for a single-region operator, blended against after-tax debt at 35% target leverage.

Terminal value

Gordon growth at 2.5%, cross-checked against an exit multiple. This is the part of every DCF that deserves suspicion, and I have put the number on the page rather than buried it: 77% of the enterprise value is terminal. We are not really valuing five years of cash flow. We are valuing an assumption about the year 2031 and beyond, and then decorating it with a forecast.

04

One number is a guess. Ten thousand is a decision.

A sensitivity table shows what happens if one assumption is wrong. Reality is that several are wrong at once, and not independently. Systems that grow admissions also tend to lever their fixed cost base, so growth and margin move together — modelling them as independent would quietly understate both tails.

So the simulation draws a shared operating quality factor first, then layers idiosyncratic noise on growth, terminal margin, capex intensity, WACC and terminal growth. Ten thousand trials, the same seed every time so the page does not change its answer when you refresh it.

That is the finding that should change behaviour. A median of $1.49B against a $1.45B ask reads like a green light until you see that the tenth and ninetieth percentiles sit roughly $900M apart — about 63% of the purchase price. No amount of extra spreadsheet detail closes a gap that wide, because the gap is not coming from arithmetic. It is coming from genuinely not knowing what a hospital's margins look like in 2032.

05

The same asset, a different buyer

A DCF answers "what is this worth?" A sponsor asks a narrower question: "what can I pay and still return 20% to my LPs?" Those are not the same question, and the difference is not a rounding error.

The model below levers the business, sweeps every dollar of free cash flow against the debt, and exits on a multiple. Because entry debt is set by the leverage multiple rather than the price, exit equity does not move with what you pay — so the affordable price solves directly rather than by iteration.

At the ask, a sponsor earns about 15.6% — respectable, and not what they raised the fund to do. To hit 20% they need to buy at roughly $1.35B. The strategic buyer, discounting the same cash flows at a lower cost of capital and able to fold back-office and contracting into an existing platform, gets to $1.49B on the same operating assumptions.

That $140M spread is not a modelling artefact. It is the deal. It tells you who wins this auction, roughly what it clears at, and what the loser's walk-away number is.

06

What I would actually tell the committee

  1. Bid, but not at the ask. Open at $1.38B — inside the sponsor's ceiling, so the competing bid has to stretch, and comfortably inside our own range. The DCF supports more; the distribution does not support paying more on the DCF alone.
  2. Put the disagreement in the structure, not the price. The single widest input is the terminal margin, and the thing most likely to move it is payer mix. So fund $70–90M of the consideration as an earn-out tied to commercial-payer share holding above its current level for eight quarters. If the seller believes their own forecast, this is cheap for them to accept. If they refuse, that refusal is information.
  3. Set the walk-away at $1.52B and write it down before the process starts. Above that number we are underwriting more than $150M of present-value synergy, and we have not yet named a single one with an owner and a date against it.
  4. Do the synergy work before the second round, not after. The whole case for paying above sponsor economics rests on integration value. Right now that is a belief. It needs to become a schedule.

What would change my mind

  • A payer contract renewal that locks commercial mix for three or more years — that collapses the widest part of the distribution and justifies bidding closer to $1.5B.
  • Deferred maintenance capex materially above the 5.5% run-rate. Hospitals hide this, and it comes straight out of terminal value.
  • Any sign the sponsor is buying a platform rather than an asset. Platform buyers pay for strategic optionality that this model cannot see, and they will outbid us.
07

Where this model is weakest

A case study that only lists its strengths is marketing. Four things here would not survive a real diligence process without more work:

  • One blended margin hides four hospitals. Systems are rarely uniform — typically one site carries the others. A site-level build would change the capex profile and probably reveal a divestiture candidate.
  • The terminal value is doing 77% of the work. I have flagged it rather than fixed it. A fade model, stepping returns down toward the cost of capital, would be more defensible than a single perpetual growth rate.
  • Normal distributions have thin tails. Healthcare risk is not symmetric — reimbursement changes and malpractice events are one-sided. A skewed or fat-tailed draw would widen the downside more than the upside.
  • The cash sweep is generous. Real credit agreements have mandatory amortisation, cash-flow sweeps with step-downs, and covenant headroom that forces equity cures. Each of those pushes the sponsor's affordable price lower, not higher.
