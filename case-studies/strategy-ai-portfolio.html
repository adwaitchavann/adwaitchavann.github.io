Case 03 — Strategy & Allocation

Twelve AI bets, one budget

Every prioritisation deck ends with the same 2×2 and the same advice: do the high-value, high-feasibility things first. That advice is not wrong. It is just not an answer — and at $18M, the difference is worth 46%.

DisciplineCapital allocation
MethodsRisk-adjusted NPV · 0/1 knapsack
DecisionWhich nine of twelve
Read8 minutes
01

The problem with the 2×2

A diversified industrial manufacturer has twelve candidate agentic-AI initiatives and $18M to spend on them. Each has been scored for value and feasibility. The workshop output is the familiar grid, and the familiar conclusion: start top-right.

Here is what that grid cannot tell you. It has no budget in it. It treats a $0.9M document-retrieval tool and a $5.2M digital twin as two dots of equal weight. It has no view on whether five expensive bets beat nine cheap ones. And it silently assumes that "value" means the number in the value column, rather than that number multiplied by the chance you ever see it.

A 2×2 ranks things. A budget forces you to choose. Those are different problems.

Reframed properly this is a constrained allocation problem — a 0/1 knapsack, solvable exactly. The interesting part is not the technique. It is how much value the usual approach leaves behind.

Method note

The twelve initiatives are an illustrative portfolio built to span a realistic range: fast cheap automation at one end, long-horizon platform bets at the other. Each carries a cost, an annual value if it works, a probability of success, and a time-to-value. Expected NPV discounts the payoff over five years at 12% and pushes it out by the implementation lag, then multiplies by the probability of success.

Costs sit on $0.1M boundaries, so the allocation is solved exactly by dynamic programming rather than approximated with a greedy ratio sort — which matters, because greedy gets this wrong precisely when the budget binds hardest.

03

Why ranking by value destroys value

At $18M, ranking by headline annual value funds five initiatives and spends $17.7M doing it. Two of them — the digital twin at $5.2M and autonomous procurement at $4.3M — absorb more than half the budget between them, at 39% and 47% probability of success and two-year and eighteen-month payback lags respectively.

The optimiser funds nine, spends $17.8M, and never touches either. Not because they are bad ideas, but because each dollar committed to them displaces roughly three dollars of expected value elsewhere in the portfolio. The opportunity cost is invisible on a 2×2 because a 2×2 has no budget line.

Push the budget slider above $28M and the two approaches converge — with enough money the ordering stops mattering, because you fund everything either way.

The dashed line is the value ranking. The gap is widest exactly where real budgets sit — tight enough to force trade-offs, loose enough that people believe the trade-offs are obvious.

04

Does buying optionality change the answer?

The obvious next move is stage-gating: spend 20% on a pilot, then commit the rest only if it works. That is a real option, and it should be worth most on the initiatives with the most uncertainty. The model says it is — the digital twin gains $2.5M of expected value and autonomous procurement $1.8M, against nine cents for the near-certain document tool.

So it should reshuffle the portfolio. Toggle Stage-gated above and it does not. Across every budget from $6M to $30M, stage-gating changes what the portfolio is worth and never changes what is in it. Expected NPV rises from $65.3M to $69.5M — about 6% — on exactly the same nine initiatives.

The moonshots gain the most and still lose, because buying an option on a poor use of capital leaves it a poor use of capital. That is a genuinely useful thing to be able to tell an executive team, and it is the opposite of what the framework literature implies.

Stage-gating is an execution discipline, not a selection criterion. Do it anyway — it is 6% for free.

05

What I would recommend

  1. Fund the nine, stage-gate all of them. $17.8M committed, roughly $69M of expected NPV. Every one carries a pilot gate at 20% of budget with a pre-agreed kill criterion written before the work starts, not after.
  2. Defer the digital twin and autonomous procurement explicitly. Not "deprioritised", which everyone reads as "dead". Deferred, with a named condition for revisiting: a higher budget, or evidence that moves the success probability above 60%. The analysis says they lose on capital efficiency today, not that they are bad ideas.
  3. Re-run the allocation every two quarters. The inputs that decide this are probabilities and time-to-value estimates, and those move faster than annual planning cycles. A portfolio set once a year is a portfolio optimised for last year.
  4. Make people estimate probability of success explicitly. The single highest-leverage change is not the optimiser — it is forcing the number that the 2×2 lets teams leave implicit. Half the value of this exercise came from asking "and what are the odds this actually works?" out loud.

Where this is fragile

  • The probabilities are judgements. They come from workshops, not data, and people are systematically overconfident about projects they proposed. I would calibrate them against the organisation's historical delivery record before trusting the third decimal place.
  • The initiatives are modelled as independent. They are not — several would share a data platform, so funding one lowers the cost of the next. Real dependencies would favour clusters over the scattered portfolio this produces.
  • Nine simultaneous initiatives may exceed delivery capacity. The budget is the constraint in this model; in most organisations the binding constraint is the number of people who can actually run a change programme. Adding a headcount constraint would likely shrink the portfolio and raise the value of stage-gating.
